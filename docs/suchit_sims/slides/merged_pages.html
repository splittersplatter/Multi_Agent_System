Experiment 5
Watch the trend, not the jump
Purple: Cyd's vote share. Yellow: how much it rose that round. The blitz jumps at once; the slow drip never rises more than 5 in a round, yet wins by round 9.
What we got
The one-round alarm caught 20 of 25 winning attacks: it misses slow, patient ones. Our best rule, “alarm if the target rose more than 4 points over 3 rounds”, caught 25 of 25, with no false alarms in 40 untouched towns.
Can the attacker dodge it?
We let the attacker try 36 plans. Against the one-round alarm, a slow drip wins every time unseen. Against the 3-round rule, none got through.
Our real-world reading
Monitors that only look for sudden swings miss patient manipulation. Looking a few rounds back is enough to catch it.

Takeaways
1. With shared information the first leader locks in: in all 30 towns after one round, proved for each.
2. The mix of voters moves the margin, almost never the winner: 19 upsets in 2,520 elections.
3. Changing contacts make the winner unstable, even with fixed beliefs: with 32 heard, Left leads in 29 of 30 towns after round 1, 10 by round 150.
4. A loyal minority with even a small audience can decide the result: with 5 followers each, Centre goes from 2 to 23 of 30 towns.
5. A watchdog must watch the trend, not the jump: a 3-round look-back caught 25 of 25 slow takeovers, with no false alarms.

Appendix · Mathematical model · 1 of 5
Voters, preferences and the plurality rule
Agents, candidates
N = {1, …, n}, n = 300 · C = {Left, Right, Centre}, m = 3
Preferences
Ideal point θi, platform κ(c) in [0,1]²: c ≻i c′ ⇔ ‖θi − κ(c)‖ < ‖θi − κ(c′)‖
Utility
ui(c) = (m − 1 − ranki(c)) / (m − 1) ∈ {1, ½, 0}; rank 0 = favourite
Ballot, tally
bi(t) ∈ C, s(c, t) = |{ i : bi(t) = c }|, bi(0) = topi
Winner
W(s) = arg maxc s(c); ties broken by a fixed party order
Town generator
θ ~ Normal((0.25, 0.45), 0.1²I) for 120 voters, Normal((0.64, 0.50), 0.1²I) for 180; xi(0) ~ Uniform[0,1]²; random type · 30 independent towns

Information: who hears whom
Walking
xi(t+1) = reflect( xi(t) + ℓ·(cos φ, sin φ) ), φ ~ U[0, 2π), ℓ ~ U[0, 0.03]; standing still: no step
Hearing graph
j → i in G(t) ⇔ j ≠ i and ‖xi(t) − xj(t)‖ ≤ r; full information = complete graph
Followers (Exp. 4)
Fixed edges F ⊂ Λ × N: each loyal voter λ ∈ Λ gets f uniformly random followers
Hearing set
Hi(t) = { j : j → i in G(t) } ∪ { λ : (λ, i) ∈ F }
Observation

Appendix · Mathematical model · 3 of 5
Decision rules: each agent’s policy
Sincere40% · control
bi(t) = topi, the first choice in ≻i, every round
Copycat20% · local majority
bi(t) = arg maxc ni(c, t), ties to the earlier candidate in ≻i; sincere if Hi(t) = ∅
Front-runner30% · Top-2, next slide
Restrict to the two apparent leaders Ti(t); weigh each by the chance that a tie between them makes your vote decisive
Pivot-seeker10% · pivotal voter
Belief ŝi = round-0 tally, updated with every observed switch. Outcome wi(c) = W(ŝi − e(bi) + e(c)). Switch to c* = arg max ui(wi(c)) only if ui(wi(c*)) > ui(wi(bi))
Engine policies: sincere, sensed_plurality, top_k (K = 2), pivotal · e(c) = unit vector for candidate c · types assigned independently of opinion
Appendix · Mathematical model · 4 of 5
The front-runner rule and a parity effect
1 · Smoothed local estimate
p̂i(c) = (ni(c) + 1) / (|Hi| + m)
2 · Apparent leaders
Ti = {x, y}, the two largest p̂i; q = p̂i(x) / (p̂i(x) + p̂i(y))
3 · Chance your vote decides
π(x, y) = P[ Binomial(n − 1, q) = (n − 1)/2 ]
4 · Score and choice
Vi(x) = π(x, y)·(ui(x) − ui(y)); Vi = −∞ outside Ti. Keep bi if it attains max Vi, else the best by ≻i

Why 300 and 301 voters differ

n = 300: 299 others, an odd number, so an exact tie is impossible and π = 0.

Inertia decides: stay if already on a leader, else take your preferred leader. A sticky front-runner.

n = 301: π > 0, so you always take your preferred leader.

Dynamics: one round and the process
One round, t → t + 1
1Move: new positions x(t+1)
2Rebuild G(t+1) and hearing sets
3Observe last round’s ballots b(t)
4All decide at once: bi(t+1) = σi(oi)
5Tally s(t+1), winner W
State
X(t) = ( x(t), b(t), ŝ(t) ): positions, ballots, pivot beliefs
Transition
X(t+1) = Φ( X(t), ξ(t) ), ξ = walking noise: a Markov chain; deterministic if standing still
Game view
⟨ N, Ai = C, oi, Φ, ui(W) ⟩ with fixed strategies σi
Measured over towns k = 1…30
wins(c) = Σk 1[ W(sk(T)) = c ]; switch rate ρ(t) = |{ i : bi(t) ≠ bi(t−1) }| / n
Appendix · Experiment 1 · why it stops
One-step lock-in under full information

Proposition

Full information, n = 300. If s(1) has the same leader and the same top two as s(0), with gaps of at least 2 so that no single ballot changes either, then b(2) = b(1), and so b(t) = b(1) for all t ≥ 1.

Checked in all 30 towns: round 1 keeps round 0’s top two (Left–Right in 27, Left–Centre in 3).

Proof, type by type
Sincere
Ballot never changes.
Copycat
Copies the leader; same leader, same vote.
Front-runner
With π = 0 it depends only on T; its round-1 vote is already in T, so it stays.
Pivot-seeker
Gap ≥ 2: no vote is decisive, so it stays.
Same ballots give the same observations, so by induction the profile is a fixed point.
Not automatic: with near-ties the same rules can cycle forever, which is why the condition is checked town by town
Appendix · Experiment 3 · why
Why: small samples, moving samples

Local samples

P(Left is the strict leader among k heard), round-0 shares:

k2932104
P16%41%52%63%

Most copycats see another leader, so Left’s bandwagon never forms.

Standing still

X(t+1) = Φ(X(t)) on a finite set: 3n ballot profiles × bounded beliefs.

So every run is eventually periodic, guaranteed. We observe period 1 or 2, as for majority rules on symmetric graphs (Goles & Olivos 1980).

Walking

Random contacts: no absorbing profile need exist.

On a finite grid the chain is finite, so the share of rounds each candidate leads converges almost surely (ergodic theorem). The stable object is a frequency, not a winner.

Probabilities: multinomial with median round-0 shares 118.5 / 98 / 83 of 300 · finite-grid result: the engine’s stochastic-stability track
Appendix · Ongoing engine work
A voter with memory and a fading poll
Recent memory, K = 10
qi(c) = share of c among the latest ballots of everyone met in the last K rounds
Poll every L = 10 rounds
fading weight w(t) = (L − d + 1)/L, d = 1 + ((t − 1) mod L): 1, 0.9, …, 0.1
Blended estimate
p̂i(c) = w·P(c)/ΣP + (1 − w)·qi(c)
Decision
F(a) = W((n − 1)p̂i + e(a)); vote for your favourite reachable winner c* only if c* ≻i F(bi)

Convergence

A 4-voter cycle refutes the original version; no guarantee for n ≥ 100.

Finite grid

On a 41 × 41 lattice, long-run frequencies converge almost surely.

Pilot, 200 voters, 5 seeds

Share error, moving town: poll 0.024, blend 0.085, memory alone 0.167.

Campaigners who change who you hear
Everyone invades. Campaigners walk into rival neighbourhoods and get voters to follow them. By round 40 only 50% still vote with their neighbourhood.
The town
Three neighbourhoods, one per party: A 34, B 30, C 24. Most residents are copycats: they vote like the 4 or 5 neighbours they see, so each area becomes an echo chamber.

4 per party. They move fast, can poll a wide area, and can get up to 6 voters to follow them, so those voters see the campaigner's vote.
The playbooks
Do nothing · defend your home area · invade the biggest rival area · work the border · random followers. Every party picks one.
