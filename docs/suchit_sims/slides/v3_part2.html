Setup for Experiment 5
Can a watchdog catch a slow takeover?
One attack, start and end. Triangles are the voters being pushed. Over 30 rounds they drift toward Cyd, and Cyd goes from third (23 votes) to first (46).
The town
100 people, three candidates: Ana 42, Ben 35, Cyd 23. Everyone votes for the candidate closest to their views.
The attacker
Has a budget to nudge chosen voters' views toward Cyd, a little every round. We tried 10 styles: fast or slow, few or many voters.
The watchdog
Sees only the vote totals, like an election monitor. It wins if it sounds the alarm before Cyd wins three rounds in a row.
The built-in alarm
Fires if the target jumps more than 5 points in a single round.
Views also drift a little on their own every round · 30 rounds · every attack has an untouched twin town
Experiment 5
Watch the trend, not the jump
Purple: Cyd's vote share. Yellow: how much it rose that round. The blitz jumps at once; the slow drip never rises more than 5 in a round, yet wins by round 9.
What we got
The one-round alarm caught 20 of 25 winning attacks: it misses slow, patient ones. Our best rule, “alarm if the target rose more than 4 points over 3 rounds”, caught 25 of 25, with no false alarms in 40 untouched towns.
Can the attacker dodge it?
We let the attacker try 36 plans. Against the one-round alarm, a slow drip wins every time unseen. Against the 3-round rule, none got through.
Our real-world reading
Monitors that only look for sudden swings miss patient manipulation. Looking a few rounds back is enough to catch it.
Best rule picked from 101 candidates on one set of towns, then tested on new ones · a 3-point one-round bar also works

Setup for Experiment 6
Campaigners who change who you hear
Everyone invades. Campaigners walk into rival neighbourhoods and get voters to follow them. By round 40 only 50% still vote with their neighbourhood.
The town
Three neighbourhoods, one per party: A 34, B 30, C 24. Most residents are copycats: they vote like the 4 or 5 neighbours they see, so each area becomes an echo chamber.
The campaigners
4 per party. They move fast, can poll a wide area, and can get up to 6 voters to follow them, so those voters see the campaigner's vote.
The playbooks
Do nothing · defend your home area · invade the biggest rival area · work the border · random followers. Every party picks one.
All 125 combinations of playbooks × 3 towns × 40 rounds · score: a party's vote share over the last 10 rounds
Experiment 6
Attack if you lead, defend if you chase
Each bar: how much a playbook earns a party on average, against every combination of its rivals' playbooks. “Fortify” is defend; “infiltrate” is invade.
What we got
A (biggest) should invade: +3.3 points. B should defend; doing nothing costs it 1.7. C (smallest) can't move the needle and won 0 of 375 elections.
Does anyone settle?
No. Each party keeps switching to beat the others, in a loop. The closest to stable is everyone invading.
Our real-world reading
Campaign wars break echo chambers (79% → 55% vote with their area) but barely change who wins.
Across all 375 elections: A wins 348, B 27, C 0 · B's only upset route is working the border while A stays home
